## The Quiet Revival of Rooftop Farming

[A] For most of the twentieth century, the flat roofs of city buildings were treated as wasted space. They held water tanks, ventilation units and little else. In recent years, however, a growing number of building owners have started to see them differently. Across many cities, roofs are being covered with soil, planted with vegetables and herbs, and managed as small farms. The movement, usually called rooftop farming, has attracted enthusiasts, investors and, increasingly, researchers who want to know whether it really delivers the benefits its supporters claim.

[B] One of the earliest systematic studies was carried out by a team at the Harbour Institute of Urban Studies. Over three summers, the researchers measured temperatures on twelve roofs, six of them planted and six left bare. On the hottest afternoons, the surface of the bare roofs reached up to 62 degrees Celsius, while the planted roofs stayed at around 34 degrees. Rooms on the top floors beneath the planted roofs were, on average, four degrees cooler, and the tenants in those rooms used air conditioning far less often. The team cautioned, however, that the effect depended heavily on the depth of the soil and on regular watering. A thin layer of dry earth, they found, did almost nothing.

[C] Food production is the second benefit usually claimed for rooftop farms. A typical commercial roof farm in the study area, covering about 400 square metres, produced between 2,000 and 3,000 kilograms of salad leaves, tomatoes and herbs each year. This is a small amount compared with a conventional farm, and nobody involved in the study suggested that roofs could feed a city. The produce does have one clear advantage, though. It can be delivered to nearby restaurants and shops within hours of harvest, which reduces transport costs and the amount of food that spoils on the way.

[D] Water is a more complicated matter. Planted roofs absorb rainwater that would otherwise rush into the drains, and during heavy storms this slows the flow into the sewage system. In the Harbour Institute study, planted roofs held back around 60 percent of the rain that fell in an average storm, which meant that the drains below were less likely to overflow. Yet the gardens themselves need irrigation in dry months, and several of the early projects relied entirely on mains water. Later farms installed tanks that collect rainwater in winter and release it slowly in summer, a change that reduced their use of mains water by roughly half.

[E] Cost is the main obstacle to wider adoption. Strengthening an old roof so that it can carry the weight of wet soil can cost more than the garden itself, and many buildings constructed before the 1970s simply cannot be adapted. For this reason, most successful projects so far have been on newer office blocks or on buildings that were designed with a planted roof in mind. Some city councils have offered tax reductions to owners who install planted roofs, but the researchers found no evidence that such incentives, on their own, persuaded owners who were unsure about the idea. Owners who did go ahead were usually motivated by lower energy bills or by the wish to make their building stand out to tenants.

[F] The social benefits of roof farms are harder to measure, although they are mentioned often. Volunteers at three of the farms said that they had made new friends among their neighbours, and a nearby primary school now brings a class of pupils to one roof each month to learn how vegetables grow. The researchers pointed out, however, that these accounts came from people who had chosen to take part, so they may not represent the views of everyone who lives near a roof farm. A neighbour who is bothered by noise from the weekend work sessions, for example, was unlikely to volunteer an opinion.

[G] The authors conclude that rooftop farming is neither a miracle nor a fashion that will soon pass. It is most convincing as a way of cooling buildings and managing rainwater, and less convincing as a source of food. They recommend that future studies follow roofs for at least ten years, because none of the farms in their sample had existed for longer than five. Until that evidence is available, they suggest, cities should support new projects but avoid promising residents more than the data can show.
