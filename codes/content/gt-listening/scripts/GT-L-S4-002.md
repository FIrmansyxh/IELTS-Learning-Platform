[A] Good evening, everyone, and thank you for coming to tonight's talk at the community library. My name is Karen Delgado, and for the past nine years I've kept bees on the roof of my apartment building, right in the middle of the city. Tonight I'd like to tell you a little about urban beekeeping and what you need to know if you're thinking of trying it yourself.
[A] People often ask me how I got started. Some assume I grew up on a farm, but in fact I'd never even seen a beehive until I was in my thirties. What happened was that a neighbor of mine kept bees, and one summer she asked me to look after her hives while she was away. By the end of that summer, I was completely hooked.
[pause 2.0s]
[A] Let me begin with a few facts about the bees themselves. A healthy colony is much bigger than most people imagine. In winter, a hive may contain only around ten thousand bees, but in the middle of summer, a single hive can contain up to sixty thousand.
[A] Now, you might expect bees in the city to struggle compared with bees in the countryside. Surprisingly, the opposite is often true. Many city hives actually produce more honey than rural ones. The main reason is the sheer variety of flowering plants in parks, gardens, and along streets. In the countryside, large areas are often planted with a single crop, which flowers for only a few weeks.
[pause 2.0s]
[A] If you're thinking of keeping bees on a roof, as I do, there are some special considerations. Heat can be a problem in summer, so you'll need some shade. But the biggest danger on a rooftop is strong wind. A hive that isn't secured can be blown over in a storm, so it must be strapped down firmly and protected by a screen or wall.
[A] Next, let's look at some of the basic equipment. The first thing every beekeeper needs is a smoker. This produces cool smoke, which calms the bees and makes them much less likely to sting while you're working. Then there's the veil, which is a hat with fine netting that protects your face, the part of the body where a sting is most unpleasant. And finally, there's the hive tool, a small metal bar that you use to separate the frames, because the bees glue everything together with a sticky substance.
[pause 2.0s]
[A] Of course, urban beekeeping does have its challenges. Many people expect disease to be the main problem, but in my experience it's no more common in the city than in the countryside. And theft of hives, which you sometimes read about, is very rare. In my view, the two biggest challenges are these. The first is neighbors who worry about being stung, so it's important to talk to them before you start, and perhaps offer them a jar of honey.
[A] The second is finding a reliable source of water. Bees need a lot of water on hot days, and if you don't provide it, they'll go looking for it, often in your neighbors' swimming pools or bird baths, which doesn't help your relationship with them.
[pause 2.0s]
[A] So, how do you get started? My strongest advice is not to buy any bees until you've done a proper course. A good beginners' course lasts about eight weeks, with one evening session a week, plus a few practical sessions at a teaching hive.
[A] As for money, it's not as expensive as people fear. You can expect to spend roughly four hundred dollars on a hive, the basic equipment, and your first colony of bees. After that, the running costs are quite low.
[A] Once your bees are settled, you'll need to check on them regularly. During spring and summer, when the colony is growing fast, I'd recommend inspecting your hive once a week. In winter, you should hardly open it at all, because the bees need to keep warm.
[pause 2.0s]
[A] Finally, I'd encourage all of you to join the City Beekeepers Association. They don't give away free hives, I'm afraid, and they don't sell honey on your behalf. But they do run an excellent mentoring scheme, where every new beekeeper is paired with an experienced member for their first year. That support made all the difference for me.
[A] Thank you very much for listening. I've brought some honey from my own hives for you to taste, and I'm happy to answer any questions.
