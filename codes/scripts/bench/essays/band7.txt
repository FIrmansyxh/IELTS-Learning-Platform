Urban congestion has become one of the defining challenges of modern cities, and governments must decide how to allocate limited transport budgets. While some argue that new roads are the most direct remedy, I would contend that investing in public transport is the more effective long-term strategy, provided that essential road maintenance is not neglected.

The strongest argument for public transport lies in its efficiency. A single metro line can move tens of thousands of passengers an hour, a volume that would require many additional lanes of traffic to match. Moreover, experience in several countries suggests that new roads often generate additional demand, since drivers who previously avoided congested routes are encouraged to return, and the improvement quickly disappears. Public transport, by contrast, breaks this cycle by offering a genuine alternative to private cars.

Equally important are the social and environmental benefits. Affordable and reliable services widen access to employment and education for low-income residents, who might otherwise be excluded by the cost of owning a vehicle. At the same time, replacing car journeys with rail or bus travel lowers emissions and noise, which in turn reduces the health costs borne by the whole community.

Nevertheless, it would be unrealistic to abandon roads altogether. Freight, emergency services and rural communities depend heavily on them, and well-maintained surfaces also keep buses running on time. A balanced budget should therefore protect existing roads while directing the bulk of new spending towards integrated public transport.

In conclusion, prioritising public transport offers cities a more sustainable and equitable solution, and the limited funds saved from road building can be used to keep essential routes safe.
